1.1The founding premise.

Roofbuddy was founded in November 2021 in Auckland, New Zealand. The founding premise was straightforward: getting a good roofing quote in this country is harder than it should be. Homeowners ring around, wait for callbacks that don't come, receive quotes in formats that are impossible to compare, and eventually make a decision that's poorly informed because they have no independent reference point. They trust whoever showed up and sounded confident.

That's not a market. That's a gap.

The gap was infrastructure. There was no trusted, technology-enabled intermediary in the New Zealand roofing market. No single point of contact who could aggregate demand, vet suppliers, manage the quote process end to end, and stand behind the outcome. Roofbuddy was built to fulfil that marketplace function and occupy that middle ground.

The company's own summary of its position: "The greatest roofing, sales, tech, pricing aggregation company in New Zealand." That's not boasting. That's a category claim. There is no direct competitor doing what Roofbuddy does at this scale anywhere in the world.

1.2The market opportunity.

New Zealand has approximately 2 million residential homes. Every roof needs replacing roughly every 50 years. That's 40,000 reroofing jobs per year, systemically, just from the baseline replacement cycle, before you factor in storm damage, insurance claims, property sales, or deterioration. The volume is enormous and it replenishes itself continuously.

What was missing wasn't demand. What was missing was a company willing to organise the supply side, apply technology to the matching process, and take responsibility for the outcome rather than simply referring and walking away like other platforms and directories.

Roofbuddy works with over 100 roofing companies throughout New Zealand. The company doesn't just match homeowners to roofers. It provides independent advice, manages the quote process, conducts third-party quality assurance on completion, and backs the entire transaction with a Customer Warranty of up to $50,000 if a roofer fails to complete. That warranty is not a marketing line. It has been activated. The Laken Wahene case, which you will encounter in your training, saw a roofer go out of business mid-job. Roofbuddy activated the guarantee, brought in new roofers, and completed the job. The client's outcome was protected. That is the product.

Full Customer Warranty terms are published at https://roofbuddy.co.nz/customer-warranties. Know where they live so you can point a client to them in a conversation.

1.3What we do for our two customers.

Roofbuddy serves two customers simultaneously. This is important to understand because it shapes how you talk about the company and the service.

For homeowners

The client gets three to four comparable quotes from vetted roofers. They get independent advice throughout the process, which means someone in their corner who isn't trying to sell them a particular roofing product. They get a single point of contact, so they're not chasing multiple service providers. They get third-party QA on completion. They get the Customer Warranty assuring the quoted works completed at the quoted price if the service provider is unwilling or unable to complete the job.

What that means in practice: they don't have to become a roofing expert to make a good decision. We do the expert work on their behalf.

For roofers in the network

Roofers in the Roofbuddy network receive qualified leads. These are homeowners who have already expressed genuine intent and been screened and had their requirements fully specified. The roofer's cost to market drops. They don't waste time on information-gathering enquiries or price checking on time consuming quotes. They arrive at jobs where a real scope of work exists and a real decision is being made. They also benefit from network vetting: being part of the Roofbuddy marketplace signals to the market that they've been assessed and approved. That protects their reputation.

"We make roofing easy." That's the company tagline and it's the product from the client's perspective. Everything in your role as a consultant should serve that statement.

1.7The company values.

The company's six values are: Morale, Performance, Ambition, Accuracy, Continuous Improvement, Esprit De Corps.

Read those carefully, they are not aspirational statements. They are descriptions of how this team is expected to operate day to day.

Morale is the foundation. Sales is hard — the team that maintains morale through difficulty outperforms the team that doesn't. Performance follows morale, not the other way around.

Performance is the output that everything else exists to produce. We are measured on results — deals closed, pipeline worked, clients served well, 5 star reviews submitted, runs on the board — not effort or intention. Performance means owning your numbers honestly, holding yourself to a measurable standard and delivering consistently in all conditions. The systems in this manual exist to make performance repeatable and inevitable; not accidental or occasional.

Ambition means hunger for more, applied honestly to your own results. Not envy of someone else's number. Hunger for your own next milestone.

Accuracy is the operational standard. Wrong specs, wrong measurements, wrong follow-up times all kill deals and damage relationships. Accuracy is not pedantry, it is professionalism.

Continuous improvement is the orientation. The 1% improvement principle: small, repeatable improvements compound over a full year into a measurably different consultant. Not transformation, not overnight identity change. Steady gain in how you do every interaction — iterate and learn from your mistakes, that's how we built this business.

Esprit de corps is the team posture. You are not in the field alone, you are part of a unit and we are special. Other consultants are not your competitors, they are your colleagues, mentors, trainees and friends. Help and be helped, in both directions.

The 5-5-8 framework

2.1Before the framework: Two principles.

Sales discipline is a deep, deep ocean. The framework in this chapter and the three that follow only scratches the surface, but if you learn it and practise it consistently, it will put you in the top 1% of direct salespeople in New Zealand. If you keep practising it across a career, it will set you up for a lifetime of success.

Sales people are made, not born - nobody walks into this role already great at it and nobody here has had that experience. The senior consultants you will meet at Roofbuddy were not naturally gifted at this on day one - they were committed enough to work the framework consistently for long enough to become excellent. The path is open to you. The work is the price.

A sales engagement should always feel natural but be structured - hold both of those at once. The structure is invisible to the client. What they experience is a calm professional who is easy to talk to and who guides them through a clear process. What is happening underneath is the framework, working as designed.

2.2The single organising architecture.

Everything you are taught at Roofbuddy fits inside one framework: the 5-5-8.

5 Steps to the Sale 5 Impulses 8 Steps to Success

These three pillars are the architecture of your work. They are not separate training modules that you pick from depending on the situation. They are three interconnected systems that operate simultaneously at every appointment, every day. A consultant who understands only the 5 Steps but ignores the 5 Impulses and the 8 Steps will produce inconsistent results. A consultant who lives inside all three pillars and understands how they reinforce each other will outperform consultants with more natural ability who don't have the system.

This is worth sitting with. The 5-5-8 is not a formula where you follow Step A, then Step B, then deploy Impulse C. It's a mental model that shapes everything: how you prepare before an appointment, how you walk up to a front door, how you handle a hesitant client, how you talk about price, how you end your day. The pillars are always present.

2.3The 5 steps to the sale.

The 5 Steps are the sequential architecture of a sales appointment. They don't change. Introduction, Presentation, Short Story, Close, Rehash. Every appointment moves through these steps in this order. The pace varies. The content adapts to the client in front of you. But the structure is fixed.

Chapter 3 of this manual covers each step in depth, with scripts, mechanics, and worked examples. Read the overview here to understand the shape of the system before you go deep on each step.

The Introduction establishes the relationship before any business conversation happens. Trust is built here, or it isn't built at all.

The Presentation is where you explain the Roofbuddy process and identify the specific needs of this client. You're not presenting a generic pitch. You're presenting a service that solves their particular problem, which you discover by asking questions during this step.

The Short Story is a targeted credibility moment. You deploy a real example of someone in a similar situation whose outcome was protected by choosing Roofbuddy. It turns an abstract promise into a concrete reference point.

The Close is not a single moment at the end of the appointment. It is a posture that runs through the entire interaction, built through dozens of minor agreements, with the formal close arriving as the natural conclusion of a process the client has already been saying yes to.

The Rehash formalises the sale. You repeat the key points, confirm the next steps, and cement the client's confidence in their decision. This step prevents buyer's remorse and sets up the ongoing relationship.

2.4The 5 Impulses.

The 5 Impulses are the psychological levers that drive buying decisions. They are:

Jones Sense of urgency Greed Fear of loss Indifference

These are not manipulation techniques. They are accurate descriptions of why people make decisions. Understanding them makes you a better consultant because you can recognise which impulse is active for a particular client at a particular moment and speak directly to it.

Jones: The impulse to keep pace with neighbours, peers, and social reference groups. This is "keeping up with the Joneses" as a sales principle. It is especially powerful in established suburbs and neighbourhoods where community norms are visible and social comparisons are constant. When you use a Short Story about another client in a similar neighbourhood who had their roof done through Roofbuddy, you are activating the Jones impulse, you're normalising the decision.

Sense of Urgency: The impulse that responds to the recognition that delay has a cost. A roof that's deteriorating gets more expensive to repair every month it's left. Timeline slots with vetted roofers fill up. The sense of urgency is not manufactured panic, it's an accurate observation about a real situation.

Greed: The impulse toward value. Not in the negative sense, but in the accurate sense: clients want to feel they are getting the best possible outcome for their money. The Roofbuddy model delivers genuinely competitive quotes from a vetted network and that value proposition is real. Greed, here, is the client's entirely reasonable desire to not overpay, and perhaps get something they don't feel like they are supposed to have on top as a sweetener.

Fear of Loss: The impulse that responds to risk. The client who has heard about a roofer going out of business mid-job, or a roof that failed two years after installation, feels this acutely. The Customer Warranty up to $50,000 speaks directly to this impulse. The Laken Wahene case and the Mr Walton case (detailed in Chapter 3) are your most powerful tools for activating and then resolving fear of loss.

Indifference: This is a professional posture, not a genuine attitude. A consultant who projects complete comfort with a client not proceeding is a consultant who does not appear desperate. Desperation kills deals. Calm, confident indifference creates a psychological dynamic in which the client is more, not less, inclined to trust your recommendation. You care about their outcome. You do not need their business.

2.5The 8 steps to success.

The 8 Steps are the behavioural and attitudinal standards that govern how you operate as a professional. They are:

1Have a positive attitude
2Maintain a positive attitude
3Be on time
4Be prepared
5Work your pipeline correctly
6Work a full day
7Know why you are here and where you are going
8Take control
1-2
Notice that two of the eight steps are about attitude. Not because attitude is a soft concept, but because it is the hardest thing to maintain consistently. It is easy to have a positive attitude on a Monday morning or the day after a big close. It is harder after three back-to-back rejections or a cancelled appointment. Step 2 exists because Step 1 is not enough - having a positive attitude is a starting position, maintaining it is the professional discipline.
3
Be on time is non-negotiable. A consultant who is late to an appointment has already damaged the relationship before the Introduction begins. Punctuality signals respect and signals organisation. It signals that you are the kind of person whose commitments can be trusted.
4
Be prepared means you have reviewed the lead, understood the context, thought about likely questions and concerns, and arrived with everything you need. A consultant who improvises because they didn't prepare is drawing on a smaller resource than a consultant who prepared thoroughly. The appointment is not the time to figure out what you're doing.
5
Work your pipeline correctly means understanding the difference between leads that are ready to move and leads that need time, and managing each appropriately. It means timely follow-up, accurate notes, and no leads falling through gaps because you forgot to call back. We close all the loops.
6
Work a full day. The hours you put in determine the number of sales you get. More time on the tool, more closes - there is no shortcut around this. To get good in this domain you need to spend the 10,000 hours fully committed to the craft. The juice will be worth the squeeze but putting in the hours is non-negotiable.
7
Know why you are here and where you are going. This is the goal-setting step. If you don't have a specific income target, a specific lifestyle target, or a specific career target, your effort has no direction. Purpose is what allows you to sustain performance through difficulty - know your why, keep it visible and continuously reinforce it to yourself.
8
Take control. The client is not leading the appointment, you are. This is not about being domineering. It's about professional confidence: you understand the process better than they do, you know where this conversation needs to go, and your job is to guide them there with warmth and clarity. An appointment that drifts is an appointment that doesn't close.

2.6How the three pillars interact.

The 5 Steps tell you what to do and when to do it. The 5 Impulses tell you why clients decide. The 8 Steps tell you how to operate as a professional regardless of the appointment you're in. A consultant operating well is using all three at every appointment and in every aspect of life.

Worked example. You're walking up to a property. The 8 Steps frame your attitude (positive attitude, maintained), your preparation (you've reviewed the lead), and your punctuality (you're three minutes early). The 5 Steps frame the structure you're about to follow (Introduction first). The 5 Impulses are running in the background - you're already noticing the roof on the neighbour's house is new (Jones cue), you're carrying the right energy for Sense of Urgency, and the warranty story is queued up for Fear of Loss if it's relevant.

You don't think about each pillar separately, the framework becomes the way you operate. That's what mastery looks like and it takes years of practice to get there. The good news is that it's fun too. The reps you put in during your first 90 days are the foundation for everything that follows.
